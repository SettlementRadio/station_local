# 12-crossings.md — the long scattering & the work of arrival

> **Cornerstone file — migration after departure: generation ships, shipborn societies, landfall,
> founding and the settlements that did not hold.** `10-history.md` gives the large historical
> sequence and `11-earth.md` holds the remembered origin; this file supplies the durable practices,
> objects and recorded withdrawals from which crossing and founding stories are built. See
> `README.md` for the authoring conventions.

## Leaving from somewhere already new

The long scattering did not run only from Earth outward. Once the first settlements could build
ships and spare people, they launched departures of their own. A family might leave a world their
grandparents had struggled to green for another place known only through surveys. A station might
fund a colony to secure a route or relieve crowding. A faith community, cooperative or commercial
charter might gather passengers from several worlds and discover during the crossing that they had
agreed on a destination but very little else.

Every departure therefore carried more than one idea of home. Earth remained the distant origin,
but the practical memories were newer: a Concordance garden, a station corridor, a frontier
workshop, the sound of rain on a world humanity had made green. Later founders did not see
themselves as repeating the first diaspora. They were leaving one human world to make another, and
they knew exactly how much could be lost between those verbs.

The largest generation ships were not vehicles with unusually long passenger lists. They were
towns under thrust. Children were born into work rosters and neighbourhood disputes their parents
had once considered temporary. Schools taught both ship maintenance and the intended destination,
though the destination was an image assembled from survey records rather than a remembered place.
By landfall, the departure generation, the shipborn and those trained specifically for arrival
could hold sharply different views of what the mission had promised.

### The Long Patience {#ship:long-patience}

The generation ship *The Long Patience* was built as a chain of rotating neighbourhood drums around
a workshop spine. Its registry treated people born aboard as citizens of the ship, not passengers
awaiting a real home, and recorded *Long Patience* itself as their birthplace. That choice became a
model for later ship registries and a quiet political statement: a life in transit was still a
life, not a gap between meaningful places.

The ship's school used a destination wall covered with maps, crop plans and children's drawings of
the expected sky. New survey packets replaced the technical sheets while the drawings stayed.
Former pupils remember the wall less for its accuracy than for the constant argument over whether
the local sun had been coloured correctly. The answer changed whenever a cleaner image arrived.

### The Landing Compact {#landing-compact}

Before a generation ship committed to landfall, its adult community renewed a Landing Compact.
The document assigned provisional work, set rules for common stores, affirmed the Settlement
Compacts and stated how the settlement could revise decisions made aboard. It also required a
recorded dissent from anyone who believed the site should be refused. Arrival was too consequential
to be remembered later as unanimous merely because the ship could not turn back easily.

The dissent pages are often the most useful part of a founding archive. Some objections proved
wrong, some embarrassingly right, and some concerned matters nobody at landfall could yet measure.
Mature settlements read them without treating doubt as betrayal. A founder who wanted another site
could still spend the rest of their life building well on the one chosen.

## The first work on the ground

Landfall was not a single moment. A survey craft arrived, cargo followed, temporary habitats were
sealed, water and soil were tested again, and people moved down only as quickly as the settlement
could keep them alive. On marginal worlds, part of the population remained aboard while the ground
crew learned which assumptions had survived contact with the place. The first view from an open
ridge might enter legend; the first maintenance report usually shaped more lives.

### The Second Map {#second-map}

The Second Map is the first map redrawn from work on the surface rather than remote survey. It marks
the ground that flooded, the slope machinery could not climb, the wind corridor nobody predicted,
the useful water that lay elsewhere and the landing place that looked sensible from orbit. Every
founding archive keeps both maps when it can: the confident first one and the corrected second.

Children enjoy finding the mistakes. Planners use them more soberly. The Second Map is the reason a
settlement street may bend around an obstacle that no longer exists or a civic building may stand
well away from the centre it was meant to occupy. A mature city carries early error in its shape
long after the error itself has been repaired.

### The First Door {#first-door}

The First Door is the earliest certified pressure-safe entrance made for common use at a founding.
It may have belonged to a shelter, clinic, store or connecting passage. When the first structure is
replaced, the door is kept and built into a public room if its condition allows. Some now open onto
an archive, a cupboard or a wall because the buildings around them changed beyond recognition.

Founding plays use the door for the arrival scene. Children queue on one side carrying absurdly
small toolboxes, an adult checks an imaginary seal, and the youngest child opens it. The ceremony
is solemn until somebody forgets which way the old door swings. On settlements without a surviving
door, a painted outline marks where it would have stood.

### The Founding Table {#founding-table}

The Founding Table marks the first communal meal made entirely from dependable local production
rather than stores carried in. The achievement may come from a field, a greenhouse, a vat or a
mixture of systems. The menu is preserved even when it was plain, badly seasoned or universally
disliked. Later celebrations improve it, then argue about whether improvement misses the point.

The Table is about independence, not abundance. A settlement can still depend on imported parts,
medicine and knowledge while feeding itself. The original serving vessel is often kept beside the
menu, patched and too fragile for use. On old settlements, cooks complain that archivists have
preserved the worst pot in the world merely because nobody had made a better one yet.

## The ship becomes a settlement

Generation ships were too valuable to preserve untouched. Their stores became clinics and
workshops. Hull plates became roofs, pressure doors and retaining walls. Reactors served the new
settlement; cables disappeared into its first streets; habitat modules became rooms people later
forgot had ever moved. Dismantling the ship was both practical necessity and a declaration that
arrival would hold.

This produced arguments that lasted well beyond the founding generation. Some wanted the vessel
kept as refuge and record. Others saw a warehouse of material standing beside people who needed
homes. Most settlements compromised without satisfying either side, preserving a control room,
nameplate or small hull section while consuming the rest. The ship survived as a thousand useful
pieces and one labelled relic.

### The Founding Shelf {#founding-shelf}

The Founding Shelf is the compact display every established settlement archive tries to maintain:
the Landing Compact, both early maps, a first ration book, a local-made tool and the last recognisable
part deliberately removed from founding use. The objects are not required to be grand. A scorched
connector with clear provenance is preferred to an impressive object supported only by family
tradition.

School groups are asked which item mattered most. They usually choose the ship part until the
archivist explains the ration book, after which they choose the food. Adults tend to choose the
Second Map. Engineers inspect the connector despite being told not to touch the display. The Shelf
works because it turns founding from a speech into a set of things people can disagree over.

Ship names persist after the material is gone. They become districts, schools, family names,
working songs and the ship-names given to children on Ship Naming Day. A person may carry the name
of a vessel whose route nobody can now reconstruct. The ship remains socially present even when
its final plate was recut generations ago.

## The settlements that did not hold

A failed settlement is not necessarily a grave. Many ended by organised withdrawal: the water
could not be sustained, the soil never settled, the route stopped paying, the founding debt became
impossible, or too many people left for the remaining community to keep every system running.
Residents moved to another world or folded into a station population. The place ended; the people
continued, carrying its recipes, grudges and name into households elsewhere.

Other endings were harsher. A settlement could lose contact and never be found, or leave a final
record that did not say whether evacuation succeeded. Historians distinguish *withdrawn*,
*absorbed*, *failed with survivors* and *unknown*. The word *lost* is used carefully. Brekka Voss's
rediscovery made the danger plain: a place can be lost to the network while its people remain very
much present to themselves.

### The Orchard Withdrawal {#orchard-withdrawal}

The Orchard Withdrawal is the best-known account of a successful abandonment. An agricultural
settlement whose name survives in conflicting forms concluded that its water cycle could not
support another generation. The community left by an agreed plan, carrying seed stock, records,
tools and every resident to receiving settlements. No dramatic rescue arrived; the achievement was
accepting the evidence before crisis made the decision for them.

Its final manifest contains a long argument over a communal cooking pot. The pot was removed as
needless mass, restored after several households objected, removed again, and finally travelled in
a family's personal allowance. The pot is now lost. The argument survives in all copies and is the
part most often performed on radio, because even an exemplary evacuation still had a kitchen
committee.

### The Unfinished Roll {#unfinished-roll}

The Unfinished Roll is the Archivist's Conclave record of settlements known to have ended or fallen
out of verified contact. Each entry gives the last reliable status and uses the narrowest conclusion
the evidence supports. *Unknown* is not silently changed to *dead*. *Withdrawn* does not become
*failed* because a later historian dislikes the site. When a world is rediscovered or an evacuation
record arrives, the old entry remains beside the correction.

### The Last Key {#last-key}

In an organised withdrawal, the last person through the main settlement access carries its manual
key or control tag away. The object is called the Last Key even when it is a lever, coded plate or
tool made for one particular lock. It proves nothing about ownership. It marks that somebody closed
the place deliberately rather than simply vanishing.

Last Keys are kept by receiving communities and brought out when former residents gather. Many no
longer open anything; some never did after the final seal was set. Children descended from a
withdrawn settlement are allowed to hold the key and are invariably surprised by its weight. The
old people then argue about who actually carried it, which is how everyone knows the community
survived well enough to disagree.

## Canon facts

1. **Most later settlements were founded from worlds and stations that were already settled, so
   the long scattering carried newer human homes outward as well as the memory of Earth.**
2. **Generation ships developed departure, shipborn and landfall generations with different claims
   on the mission; arrival decisions were civic decisions, not merely navigation.**
3. **The generation ship *The Long Patience* recorded the ship itself as the birthplace of people
   born aboard, treating life in transit as citizenship rather than a pause before arrival.**
4. **The Landing Compact set provisional work, common stores and rights before landfall, and kept
   recorded dissent so a site choice could not later be remembered as unanimous.**
5. **The Second Map corrected orbital survey with surface experience; founding archives keep it
   beside the confident first map, and mature streets still bend around some of its old corrections.**
6. **The First Door is the earliest certified common entrance from a founding, preserved in a public
   room when possible and used by children in arrival plays.**
7. **The Founding Table marks the first communal meal made entirely from dependable local production;
   its original plain menu is preserved and improved versions are argued over.**
8. **The Founding Shelf keeps a Landing Compact, early maps, a ration book, a local-made tool and the
   last recognisable ship part removed from use, favouring clear provenance over impressive relics.**
9. **A settlement may end through organised withdrawal or absorption without its people dying;
   historians distinguish those outcomes from failure with survivors and an unknown fate.**
10. **The Orchard Withdrawal succeeded by moving every resident, seed stock, records and tools after
    the water cycle proved unsustainable; its final manifest preserves a long dispute over a
    communal cooking pot.**
11. **The Unfinished Roll records the last verified status of ended or unreachable settlements and
    keeps *unknown* distinct from *dead*, with later corrections beside the original entry.**
12. **The Last Key is the manual key, tag or tool carried out during an organised withdrawal; former
    residents keep it as evidence that the settlement was deliberately closed.**
