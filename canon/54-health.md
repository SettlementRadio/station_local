## The two-room clinic

On most settled worlds, medicine is a clinic of two rooms and a medic who is also something else —
hydroponicist, teacher, water engineer — because no skill travels alone out there. The clinic holds
what a clinic can hold: the diagnostic bench, the drug locker, the drilled competence of one or two
people, and the casebooks. Serious medicine — the surgeries that need a theatre, the drugs that need
a fabricator, the conditions that need a specialist's hands — lives only in the dense core and on
the largest stations, weeks of transit away. So the frontier's first medical art is triage of a
particular kind: not *who first*, but *here or away* — what can be managed in these two rooms, and
what begins the old, hard conversation about a ship.

Because the nearest specialist answers in weeks, frontier medicine is protocol medicine. The
medic's shelf of checklists and drilled procedures is not a poor cousin of core practice; it is its
own discipline, honed exactly for the case where the expert cannot be reached and the second chance
does not come. Every settled adult carries the basic competences — air, wounds, resuscitation — as
part of the common schooling, and a settlement drills its medical emergencies the way it drills its
pressure drills: bored, regular, and grateful for the boredom.

### The Open Door {#the-open-door}

The first rule of a settlement clinic is the **Open Door**: anyone physically inside the habitat is
treated with what the clinic has, whether they are a resident, a freight hand, a passenger between
ships, or someone whose papers name a world no one present has seen. The questions about employer,
ship, home settlement, and payment come after the medic's questions. At a busy port, the same narrow
bench serves people who own half a cargo and people who arrived carrying everything they possess.

The clerk works out the obligation later. A ship may repay the clinic from its medical stores, a
home settlement may accept the charge by relay, or a mutual-aid ledger may carry it until someone
can settle it. Some costs are never recovered and become part of what it means to keep a safe port.
The Open Door guarantees access to the care that is present; it does not create a specialist, a
vacant berth, or a treatment the settlement cannot supply.

## The correspondence of physicians

The profession holds itself together on the queue. A medic with a hard case writes it up — history,
readings, what was tried — and sends it to the physician-correspondence network: the standing
exchange of consult letters that rides the relays between every clinic and academy in the settled
worlds. Answers come back in weeks, from doctors under other skies who have seen something like it,
disagree with each other, and say so. The exchange is archived, appended, and argued into the relay
casebooks — the profession's slow shared memory — so every hard case treated anywhere eventually
teaches everywhere. A famous unresolved case runs for years and is followed like a serial; medics
name their favourites.

The cruelty and the dignity of the network is its timing. A consult's answer routinely arrives for
a patient who is already recovered or already buried. Physicians answer anyway, carefully, at
length — the reply was never only for this patient; it is for the casebook, and the next one. *Write
for the next case* is the first thing the correspondence teaches, and the closest thing the
profession has to a creed.

Some physicians still ride instead of write. The circuit physicians travel scheduled loops of
small settlements the way the Circuit Scholars do — a year or more to a loop, carrying medicine,
technique, fresh casebook pages, and news of who lives. The tradition descends from Verene Mere's
Silence-era crossings, and the old phrase still holds: to serve a stretch of cut-off worlds well is
*riding the Mercy Round*, and there is no higher compliment in the trade.

## Quarantine and the sealed world

In a closed habitat, air is held in common, and so is anything that rides it. Illness in a station is
an engineering event as much as a medical one: sickness moves along the ducts and corridors the
engineers know by heart, and an outbreak response looks like maintenance — sectioning, sealed
doors, the clean corridor, the numbers watched shift by shift. The oldest habit of settled public
health is architectural, and every station drill assumes it.

Ships hold quarantine at dock. The length is the port's custom — days, usually, set by the
harbourmaster and the medic together, adjusted for where the ship has been — and crews grumble
through it in dockside quarters built for exactly that grumbling. Nobody seriously argues. A sealed
dock is not unfriendliness; it is carefulness, and the settled worlds remember why. Ports signal a
closed dock with a steady amber lamp, and "riding out the amber" is every spacer's least favourite
fluency.

## The pharmacopoeia and the cold chain

Drugs divide the way everything divides out here: what a world can make, and what must cross the
dark. The basics — the analgesics, the common antibiotics, the field synthesises — most settlements
produce locally, and the *formulas* travel at broadcast speed, checked and countersigned across the
network. But the complex drugs need a core fabricator, and those cross at ship speed, in the
refrigerated holds of the Medical Transports, on schedules every clinic knows by heart. That
difference — the formula in hours, the finished drug in months — is the health gap between core and
frontier, stated as physics.

So the cold chain is watched like weather. A Medical Transport running late is front-page news on
every world it serves; clinics lend stock across neighbouring settlements on standing trust, argue
expiry margins with the seriousness the numbers deserve, and keep the shortage arithmetic — who is
owed what, which locker holds the last of it — as carefully as any Exchange House keeps its ledgers.
Asking after a neighbour world's drug locker is, on the frontier, a normal courtesy of
correspondence.

## What illness means across the lag

Between worlds, illness travels as letters. The bundle that brings word that a father is sick may be
the bundle that brings word he is well again — "by the time you read this I am recovered" is among
the most-written sentences in the settled worlds, and its darker sibling is written carefully and
read slowly. Families weigh passage against odds and against physics: weeks of transit means
arriving to the middle of a story, or its end. Some go anyway; some write instead, and the long
letters kept from an illness — read aloud at the Sending of the Dead, sometimes — are their own
quiet literature.

At home, care is communal because everything is. The household nurses its sick the way it raises
its children — in shifts, by rota, with the neighbours' claim on the work taken for granted — and a
settlement's real health system is this: the clinic at the centre, and around it the mesh of people
who notice a missed meal, a missed shift, a light left on too long. Nobody on a settled world is
sick alone unless they have worked very hard at it.

### The Passage Conference {#the-passage-conference}

A referral away begins with a **Passage Conference**, not a ticket. The patient, the local medic,
someone the patient trusts, and the ship that may carry them put the same hard facts on the table:
whether a receiving clinic has accepted the case, whether the crossing can be borne, what the ship
can support, who will travel too, and how a return might be made. The reply from the receiving
clinic may itself have taken weeks. A berth may vanish while the letters cross.

The person in greatest danger is not automatically the person sent. Some cannot survive the
journey; some can be helped locally; some would reach care too late for it to change what follows.
The decision and the reasons are written into the clinic record, including a refusal by the patient.
Families may disagree for the rest of their lives, but the record must show that the choice belonged
to a person, not to an empty line in a sailing manifest.

### Care companions {#care-companions}

When passage is chosen, the clinic plans for a **care companion** wherever a ship can spare the
berth. The companion is not turned into a medic. They keep the patient company, hold the case
papers, speak when the patient does not wish to, and learn the routines specific to that crossing.
Medical travel therefore removes two people from a small settlement more often than one, and the
departure board fills with exchanged shifts before either traveller reaches the dock.

The bags reveal which journey each person is making. The patient carries records and the few home
things that fit around them. The companion carries spare clothes, letters to deliver, requests from
the clinic, and usually too much food pressed on them by neighbours. If only one berth is available,
household voices travel in a packet the ship's crew is asked to play at agreed points on the way.

### Landing Houses {#landing-houses}

The large stations and core medical centres have **Landing Houses** for patients and companions who
arrive from elsewhere. They are plain dormitories with a common kitchen, a relay desk, lockers large
enough for travel cases, and a route board with two sides: waiting for care and waiting for a ship
home. They exist because access to a specialist means little if a patient cannot afford to live near
the clinic or remain there until a safe return passage appears.

Frontier settlements, Mutual Aid Societies, and former residents keep the houses supplied. Cupboards
acquire labels from worlds with different meal habits; the lost-property shelf fills with clinic
slips, charging leads, children's drawings, and mugs nobody will discard. People bound along the
same route share news and sometimes leave together, their treatment finished at different times but
their journey home governed by the same ship.
