# 11-earth.md — the remembered home

> **Cornerstone file — Earth as record, inheritance and founding myth.** `10-history.md` holds the
> long diaspora and `06-gazetteer.md` holds the inhabited Old System; this file covers what the
> settlements remember of Earth, what the departure ships carried, what was lost, and how a living
> world became an ancestral idea. Earth is the sole real-world exception allowed by the authoring
> contract; no real nation, city, person, institution or work is named here. See `README.md` for
> the authoring conventions.

## The home that became a story

Earth is not lost. It remains inhabited in the Old System, governed by people who regard their
world as a place to live rather than a shrine. Its weather still inconveniences commuters. Its
buildings need repair. Its public arguments are current at home and old by the time they travel the
long relay road to the settlements. The distance is so great, however, that ordinary continued life
does little to weaken the ancestral Earth carried in settlement culture.

For most people in the settled worlds, Earth is a set of first facts: the world where humanity
began, a blue surface dominated by open water, one moon, a breathable sky, living landscapes that
arose without human greening. These facts are correct. The feeling wrapped around them is less
reliable. Earth becomes the garden before work, the home before departure, the place where rain was
free and every horizon was kind. The records show crowded cities, exhausted land, inequality,
ordinary bad government and all the pressures that helped send people outward. Affection edits
without fully lying.

Earth has no political authority over the settlements. It is one distant polity among many and the
Old System belongs to neither the dense centre nor the frontier. A ceremonial message from Earth
may receive respectful airtime, but it carries no ancestral right to command. The departure was
not a delegation sent to extend a single state. It was a long accumulation of migrations whose
purposes often disagreed.

### The Blue Lesson {#blue-lesson}

The Blue Lesson is the Earth unit inside the Common Lessons taught to every settled child. It
begins with the physical world: oceans, continents, moving weather, the old moon, the diversity of
life and the difference between a native biosphere and a human-made one. It then separates three
columns on the classroom board: what the records establish, what a particular settlement believes,
and what no surviving source can answer.

Children ask practical questions adults enjoy repeating. Why was rain allowed to run into the
ground? Could people really stand beside an ocean and not collect it? Did houses open windows to
untreated air? Teachers answer patiently and then ask what seems ordinary on their own world that a
distant descendant might find impossible. The lesson is meant to teach source care as much as
ancestry.

The customary final exercise is to draw Earth without looking at a map. The pictures are
recognisably the same world and geographically hopeless: too much green, settlements visible from
space, the moon always full, clouds arranged for decoration. Teachers correct the science and keep
the pictures. School archives display old sets beside new ones, a record of how one world is
misremembered in much the same way across many others.

## What crossed with the ships

The departure ships carried no complete Earth. They carried selected tools for survival and
whatever their builders, sponsors and passengers could fit around them. Seed stock, soil cultures,
medical knowledge, engineering references, legal forms, teaching material and language records
received planned space. Music, recipes, private letters, religious objects, family photographs,
games and jokes arrived through thousands of personal decisions. Some of the most durable cultural
inheritance crossed as somebody's luggage.

### The Carrying Lists {#carrying-lists}

The Carrying Lists are the surviving departure manifests gathered from generation ships and early
colonies. There was never one master list. Some are precise cargo accounts, some passenger-made
copies, some later reconstructions based on what a settlement possessed at founding. Archivists use
them to trace a crop, tool pattern, song family or ritual back toward Earth without pretending the
line is always complete.

The Lists also show what planners expected to matter. They devote great care to machines, seed
libraries and technical instruction, then describe cultural material in phrases so broad they are
almost useless. A line marked *recreation recordings* may be all that remains of a library. A box
entered as *kitchen tools* may contain an object now treated as a museum treasure. The imbalance is
not evidence that the travellers cared only about survival. It is evidence that survival had a
shipping office and memory often did not.

The living worlds themselves are part of the carried archive. Most food crops, working animals,
garden plants and microbial cultures descend from Earth stock, altered by selection and engineering
to survive elsewhere. On a greened world, an orchard may be genetically distant from anything now
growing on Earth and still belong to the same inheritance. Native life is kept distinct by compact;
the Earth inheritance is tended, adapted and argued over as the biological toolkit humanity brought
with it.

### Earthwood {#earthwood}

Earthwood is timber whose provenance reaches back to Earth or to a plant grown aboard a departure
ship from authenticated stock. Genuine pieces are rare, usually small and repaired many times. A
handle becomes a frame, the frame becomes an instrument fitting, and the offcut is kept because
throwing it away would break the record. Museums label the chain of custody carefully; families are
more willing to accept a good story.

Most objects called Earthwood do not have complete proof. This is not always fraud. A family may
have carried the same tool for generations while losing the document that named its material. The
polite museum label is *departure-era wood, origin held by tradition*. The impolite version is what
siblings say while arguing over who inherits it.

## The ordinary things that survived

Grand histories crossed in multiple copies because institutions knew they were grand. Ordinary
life survived unevenly. A technical archive may explain an old water system in exact detail and
say nothing about what a kitchen sounded like at breakfast. The settlements know the outlines of
Earth's governments, industries and ecological crises better than they know its playground rules,
cheap meals, local insults or the small tasks people postponed on an ordinary afternoon.

### The Ordinary Box {#ordinary-box}

The Ordinary Box is a departure-era container held in the Concordance archives. Its contents are
unimportant by every formal measure: a worn cooking spoon, a door latch, school exercises, household
notes, a child's weather diary and short recordings made in rooms whose occupants are unnamed. The
box has no famous owner and no connection to a decisive event. That is why archivists value it.

The most requested recording contains people clearing a meal while a drawer repeatedly refuses to
close. No one says anything historic. Someone complains about the drawer, someone else laughs, and
water can be heard running longer than a station listener thinks reasonable. School groups are
often surprised that the past sounds irritated rather than solemn.

### The Earth Weather Set {#earth-weather-set}

The Earth Weather Set is a collection of early field recordings preserved in the station's deep
archive: rain on several roofs, wind moving through leaves, waves arriving on an open shore,
insects at dusk and a storm heard from inside a dry room. Locations and recordists were lost when
the files were copied away from their catalogue, so the sounds are evidence without geography.

The station uses the Set sparingly. Listeners on wet worlds find some of it ordinary; listeners born
inside stations can find the uncontained water unsettling. The famous argument is over one quiet
track in which a low repeated sound may be an animal, a machine or somebody shifting furniture in
another room. The archive labels it *unidentified domestic background* and refuses all improvements.

### The Lost Index {#lost-index}

The Lost Index records Earth material known only through references: books named in letters, songs
listed on damaged catalogues, images described but no longer present, family histories whose first
pages survived and last pages did not. An item enters the Index only when there is evidence it once
existed. It is not a list of everything people wish the past had contained.

Artists use the Index freely, writing new works around old absences. Archivists keep the boundary
plain. A reconstruction may become beloved and widely performed, but it does not become the lost
original through repetition. The Index preserves both the absence and the later answers to it,
because the worlds inherit what vanished as well as what survived.

## Earth in the present tense

Many things called Earth traditions are settlement traditions with an Earth ancestor. The shared
day and year count came from Earth. So did the fossil language of ships, crews and stations, the
old faiths reshaped during transit, and recipes whose ingredients have been substituted world by
world. Authenticity usually means an honest line of descent, not an unchanged form.

This produces affectionate nonsense. Settlements claim rival versions of an Earth breakfast no
departure record describes. Families insist a hand game was played exactly the same way under the
old sky. A ceremony acquires an Earth origin because nobody remembers starting it locally. The
Archivist's Conclave corrects claims when evidence matters and leaves harmless family stories alone.
Not every inherited error needs a tribunal.

New material still comes from the Old System. Music, reports, scholarship and personal messages
arrive seasons late, proof that Earth and its neighbouring habitats did not freeze when the ships
left. The old-system sessions are loved partly because their subjects are so
familiar: work, rent, weather, relatives, somebody far away. A new recording can sound ancient in
the settled worlds and entirely ordinary to the people who made it.

Travellers who have visited Earth are asked to adjudicate impossible questions. Is the sky really
that wide? Does the sea have a smell? Which settlement keeps the truest old recipe? Most answer
that Earth is too large and varied for one visit to settle anything. People are disappointed, ask
again, and then want to know what the traveller ate. Myth yields to appetite more easily than to
scholarship.

## Canon facts

1. **Earth remains inhabited and ordinary within the Old System; it is not counted among the
   settled worlds and holds no ancestral authority over them.**
2. **What settled children learn of Earth is half record and half founding myth: the physical blue
   world is well documented, while memory turns it into an easier home than it ever was.**
3. **The Blue Lesson teaches Earth's physical facts in the Common Lessons and separates what records
   establish, what a settlement believes and what no surviving source can answer.**
4. **The Carrying Lists are the incomplete surviving manifests of the departure ships, used to
   trace tools, crops and cultural material without pretending there was one master cargo.**
5. **Most settlement crops, working animals, garden plants and microbial cultures descend from
   Earth stock adapted for other worlds, while native life is kept distinct by compact.**
6. **Earthwood is authenticated or traditionally held timber from Earth or the departure ships,
   repeatedly recut into smaller objects; museums distinguish proven origin from family tradition.**
7. **The Ordinary Box in the Concordance archives preserves household objects, schoolwork and
   domestic recordings with no famous owner; its best-known recording is an argument with a drawer
   that will not close.**
8. **The Earth Weather Set in Settlement Radio's deep archive preserves rain, leaves, waves, insects
   and a storm, but the locations and recordists were lost from the catalogue.**
9. **The Lost Index records Earth works and family histories known to have existed but no longer
   preserved; later reconstructions remain labelled as new works.**
10. **Earth-derived customs include the shared day and year count, fossil words, old faiths and
    recipes, but the settlements treat honest descent rather than unchanged form as authenticity.**
11. **New music, reports and messages still arrive from the Old System, usually old by reception,
    and remind the settlements that Earth continued after the diaspora.**
12. **Travellers returning from Earth are commonly asked about oceans, weather and old customs, but
    one visit gives them no special authority over a world that large and varied.**
