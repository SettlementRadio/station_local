# 41-crime.md — inquiries, disputed cargo & the long account

> **Cornerstone file — the cases behind the law: inquiries, tribunals, smuggling, salvage disputes
> and fraud.** `40-law.md` pins the rights and principles; this file supplies the durable procedures
> and settled cases from which a court report or long-form case narrative can be built. It records
> harm and repair, never criminal method, spectacle or victims as decoration. See `README.md` for
> the authoring conventions.

## Harm in a world of manifests

Most crime in the settled worlds is not mysterious. Something promised did not arrive. Something
certified safe was not. A cargo has more than one claimant, a crew has not been paid, a salvage
operator has mistaken silence for abandonment, or a port has discovered undeclared goods after the
people responsible have already left. Distance gives an ordinary deception room to grow. By the
time the loss is visible, the goods may be consumed, divided or carried onward, and the person who
made the promise may be weeks away by ship.

That makes records part of daily survival. Manifests, maintenance books, cargo receipts, crew-share
accounts and relay acknowledgements are dull until two copies disagree. Then a clerk's careful
notation may matter more than a forceful accusation. An inquiry begins by establishing what was
expected, what was actually present and who absorbed the difference. The central question remains
the one set by the Settlement Compacts in `40-law.md`: what harm was done, and what repair will let
people continue living and trading together?

The people doing this work are rarely professional investigators. A port inquiry usually combines
a records examiner, someone who understands the cargo or system involved, and a Compact mediator.
On a small station these may be ordinary workers released from their shifts. They inspect, ask,
record and preserve; they do not chase suspects through corridors or perform for a crowd. If a case
crosses jurisdictions, each port contributes its piece and the final account is assembled over the
relays.

Fraud is treated seriously because trust substitutes for proximity. An Exchange House cannot
visit every warehouse behind a note it honours. A frontier cooperative cannot inspect parts before
committing its future income to them. A ship's crew may finish a crossing before learning that its
profit share was calculated from false accounts. The damage is usually distributed among people
who never met the person responsible: a repair postponed, a ration tightened, a worker paid late.
Crime reporting names those consequences without turning hardship into atmosphere.

## Inquiry across the lag

There is no universal police service and no court that follows a case instantly from world to
world. The first safe jurisdiction secures the record; other jurisdictions decide what authority
they recognise; the Settlement Council receives only the disputes that parties are willing and
able to carry that far. This produces untidy justice, but the untidiness is visible. Every serious
case leaves a packet of claims, replies, technical findings and proposed repair that another port
can inspect.

### The First Port Hearing {#first-port-hearing}

The First Port Hearing is the preliminary inquiry held wherever a disputed ship or cargo next
reaches safety. It does not settle ownership for every world. It records the condition on arrival,
identifies urgent needs, preserves statements and prevents disputed essentials from vanishing into
ordinary trade before the claim can be heard. Salvage crews value the hearing because it protects
honest work; original owners value it because possession at the dock is not treated as proof of
title.

The hearing is practical. Perishable cargo may have to be used rather than held, and life-support
parts cannot sit sealed while a station waits for distant argument. In those cases the port records
the use and fixes a value for later restitution. The point is not to preserve an untouched exhibit
at the cost of present harm. It is to leave a trustworthy account of what necessity required.

### The Late Word Rule {#late-word-rule}

The Late Word Rule allows a finding to be reopened when relevant testimony or records arrive after
the tribunal has finished. The later material is not automatically believed and does not erase the
work already done. It is attached to the case, tested against the existing record and answered in a
supplement. A party cannot win merely by being distant, but neither are they judged to have agreed
because the relay queue kept them silent.

The rule gives case narratives their layered shape. Listeners may hear the confident local finding,
then the missing ship's account, then the technical reply, each recorded at a different point in the
same dispute. Settlement Radio dates those layers in the live world state when it reports them; the
canon fixes only the standing right of late evidence to be heard.

### The Harm Ledger {#harm-ledger}

The Harm Ledger is the shared index of unresolved interworld claims under the Common Trade Code.
It contains a short statement of the loss, the jurisdictions involved, the status of replies and
the repair ordered or still sought. It does not publish private testimony or turn harmed people
into searchable curiosities. Ports and Exchange Houses consult it before extending credit, buying
salvage or accepting a disputed cargo.

Entry in the Ledger is not a conviction. Some claims end with no finding; others reveal an error
rather than deception. The outcome stays attached to the entry, because an accusation that travels
without its resolution is another kind of harm. Clearing a false or satisfied claim from the
working list is a small formal ceremony: the clerk reads the resolution, closes the account and
sends the corrected entry back along every relay path that carried the original.

## The cases everyone knows

The old cases endure because they settled a useful principle or left behind a detail ordinary
people remember. They are not tales of ingenious offenders. They are accounts of incomplete
records, divided responsibility and repair that arrived later than it should have.

### The Far Reach Return Case {#far-reach-return-case}

A hauler found silent near Far Reach was taken in salvage and its cargo distributed while the crew
were absent at an isolated repair shelter. The crew returned to find their ship under another
registry and their personal effects already dispersed. The appeal held that silence and temporary
absence did not by themselves prove abandonment, especially in a dark zone where neither side
could send timely word.

The salvagers had answered a genuine hazard and were not treated as raiders, but acting too quickly
had caused harm. Restitution was divided among the port that authorised distribution, the buyers
who retained recoverable goods and the salvage concern that asserted title. The crew recovered the
ship. The missing personal effects could not all be returned, and the judgement says so plainly
rather than pretending money made that part whole.

### The Breathe Easy Stores Inquiry {#breathe-easy-stores-inquiry}

During the era when Breathe Easy's first air plant was still unreliable, its maintenance accounts
showed a healthy reserve while the stores held neither the required parts nor enough credit to buy
them. The inquiry found false assurances layered over neglected checks, with responsibility spread
across administrators, suppliers and the station's own exhausted oversight. It rejected the public
hope for a single mastermind because the record did not support one.

The settlement endured a hard period of rationing and repair, then placed its essential stores
under cooperative inspection. The open store count is still read at shift handover even though the
air has been sound for generations. Residents complain about the ritual, correct the reader from
the corridor and become fiercely attentive if an item is skipped. That ordinary nuisance is the
inquiry's living result.

### The Halcyon Double Claim {#halcyon-double-claim}

The Halcyon Double Claim began when several station cooperatives presented valid-looking rights to
the same consignment of reactor seals. The cargo itself was ordinary and intact; the fraud lay in
the competing promises around it. The receiving tribunal refused to make the distant cooperatives
fight over the parts while the institution that had certified the claims stood aside.

The finding made the certifying Exchange House responsible for the first restitution, leaving it
to pursue the fraudulent seller separately. The decision became a standard lesson in financial
work: verification carries liability, not merely a fee. Halcyon remembers the affair through a
clerk's phrase in the record — "one cargo, one promise" — now printed above the claims desk where
everyone waiting can see it and nobody enjoys being reminded of it.

### The Cold Harbor Thaw Docket {#cold-harbor-thaw-docket}

When Cold Harbor's sea lanes reopen, the Thaw Docket hears the accumulated disputes over
undeclared cargo, missing levies and goods that entered without inspection during the long night.
The cases are rarely grand: cloth, preserved fruit, machine fittings, seed stock, a crate carried
for a cousin and omitted from the book. Need may explain a choice without making its costs disappear.
The tribunal asks who took the risk, who would have carried the loss and what repair is possible.

The docket sits in a working hall beside the wet docks so crews can give evidence before dispersing.
A basket of dry socks is kept by the door for witnesses who came straight off the ice. Taking a pair
does not count against anyone's case, a point the clerk repeats because newcomers always ask. The
detail is funny; the proceedings are not treated as entertainment.

### The Forge False Mark Inquiry {#forge-false-mark-inquiry}

The Forge False Mark Inquiry joined complaints from several settlements that had received heavy
parts carrying certifications they had not earned. No single dramatic failure exposed the fraud.
Repair crews noticed early wear, removed equipment from service and sent reports that looked minor
until an examiner laid them side by side. The case became famous for proving the value of complaints
too small to make a bulletin on their own.

The responsible workshops were closed, their outstanding contracts transferred and the cost of
replacement shared with the certifiers that had failed to check their work. Forge's training hall
keeps one rejected housing on an ordinary floor stand. Apprentices are asked first who lost a shift,
who lost credit and who had to repeat the work; only then are they asked what was wrong with the
part.

## Smuggling without romance

Smuggling is undeclared movement, not a single moral category. Some cargo is prohibited because it
is dangerous. Some bypasses a levy. Some violates an embargo whose justice is disputed. Some is an
ordinary gift entered wrongly by a tired crew member. A tribunal distinguishes among these because
the harm differs, but necessity does not turn concealment into heroism and a rule does not become
just merely because it exists.

The attractive version of a smuggling story centres the clever carrier and the narrow escape. The
settled worlds know the less attractive remainder: the port worker held responsible for an
unrecorded load, the cooperative whose legitimate cargo loses space, the receiving settlement left
with goods it cannot verify, the family that paid for something confiscated before delivery. Good
reporting begins there. It can examine an unjust restriction or a desperate choice without giving
the offence a set of instructions or treating consequences as scenery.

Salvage disputes require the same discipline. Salvagers perform necessary and often brave work,
but the romance of a recovered ship can hide the original crew's wages, tools, letters and cabins
inside the word *property*. The First Port Hearing asks what must be rescued before it asks what can
be claimed. The most respected salvage concerns publish the disputes they lost as well as the ones
they won, because a market built on trust needs evidence of correction.

Settlement Radio treats a court report and a case narrative differently. The report states the
claim, status, known harm and next formal step without deciding ahead of the tribunal. The long
narrative can follow the record through contradictions and late replies, but it keeps the people
affected in their own words and never makes the offender the only interesting person in the room.
The attraction is not technique. It is the slow work of finding an account that people in several
places can accept as true.

## Canon facts

1. **Interworld inquiries begin by establishing what was expected, what was present and who
   absorbed the difference; repair under the Settlement Compacts is the aim.**
2. **There is no universal police service; a port inquiry usually combines a records examiner, a
   worker who understands the cargo or system, and a Compact mediator.**
3. **The First Port Hearing records the condition and urgent use of a disputed ship or cargo at the
   first safe jurisdiction without pretending to settle ownership for every world.**
4. **The Late Word Rule allows a finding to be reopened when relevant testimony or records arrive
   after the tribunal, but the later material must be tested against the existing record.**
5. **The Harm Ledger indexes unresolved interworld claims and their outcomes under the Common Trade
   Code; an entry is a notice, not a conviction, and private testimony is not published in it.**
6. **The Far Reach Return Case held that silence and temporary absence in a dark zone did not prove
   a hauler abandoned; the crew recovered the ship, while the port, buyers and salvage concern
   shared restitution for goods already dispersed.**
7. **The Breathe Easy Stores Inquiry found that false assurances and neglected checks had left the
   old air plant without the reserve shown in its accounts; essential stores have been counted
   openly at shift handover ever since.**
8. **The Halcyon Double Claim made a certifying Exchange House responsible when several station
   cooperatives held competing claims to one consignment; verification carries liability as well
   as a fee.**
9. **The Cold Harbor Thaw Docket hears disputes over undeclared cargo when the sea lanes reopen; it
   sits beside the wet docks and keeps dry socks by the door for witnesses coming off the ice.**
10. **The Forge False Mark Inquiry joined minor reports of improperly certified heavy parts from
    several settlements and required the workshops and failed certifiers to share replacement
    costs.**
11. **Cargo fraud commonly concerns missing goods, false certification, competing claims or unpaid
    crew shares; reports focus on the postponed repair, tightened ration or late wage rather than
    the trick used.**
12. **Smuggling covers undeclared cargo ranging from prohibited goods to levy avoidance and
    disputed embargoes; tribunals judge the harm and necessity without treating concealment as
    heroism.**
13. **A salvage claim is heard only after rescue needs are met; personal effects, crew wages and
    working tools are accounted for separately from the vessel and saleable cargo.**
14. **Court reports state the claim, status, known harm and next formal step without deciding guilt
    ahead of the tribunal.**
15. **Long-form case narratives may follow contradictions and late replies, but they keep affected
    people in their own words and omit criminal method detail.**
