# 56-style.md — dress, cloth & the look of the worlds

> **Cornerstone file — what people wear across the settled worlds, and what it says.** This is the
> `fashion` domain. The economy of textiles-as-luxury is in
> `35-economy.md`; the home aesthetic and the "display of excess is vulgar" ethos are in
> `50-daily-life.md`. See `README.md` for the authoring conventions.

## Workwear first

All settled dress descends from workwear. Clothes out here are equipment before they are anything
else — thermal layers for the cold worlds, storm gear for Meridian's coasts, the close-fitting
shipsuit whose every pocket has a reason, the dock jacket built to take a tool's weight and a
cable's snag. A world's look is readable from its work and its weather the way its accent is
readable from its distance: you can place a stranger to within a few worlds by their boots. And
because the frontier ethos holds the display of excess to be vulgar, distinction went where it
could: into detail. The cut of a collar, the closures, the stitching, whose hands did the mending
and how visibly — settled dress is a quiet language of exactly such small things, fluent
everywhere, showy nowhere.

Mending is the heart of it. Cloth crosses the dark at freight prices, so a garment is kept in
service for decades, and the mending is done openly — contrast thread, deliberate patches, the
repair displayed rather than hidden. A patched jacket carries its history on its surface: this tear
from the dock, that burn from the engine room, the shoulder rebuilt by a partner's hands. "Worn
well" is a real compliment, and it is aimed at the wearer as much as the coat.

## Cloth and where it comes from

Every world's cloth names its origin. Fibre comes from what a world can grow or culture — vat-silk
on the stations, coarse crop-fibres on the agricultural worlds, real wool and cotton from the
garden worlds of the core, moving in small lots at luxury prices with the fruit. Dyes come from
local chemistry, so a world's palette is very nearly its geology: Cold Harbor's greys and sea-glass
blues, Forge's rust and char, the unlikely greens the garden worlds can afford. A trained eye reads
a bolt of cloth like a manifest — what it is made of says where it is from, and what it cost says
how far it has come.

That is why a textile is the classic heirloom of the settled worlds. A length of home-world cloth
carried across generations — re-cut, re-sewn, never discarded — outlasts the memory of the world it
came from and stands in for it. Handling a family's textile is handling their map.

## What comes out of an old coat

### The Unpicking Table {#the-unpicking-table}

When a garment can no longer take another patch, it is not thrown away. It goes to an **Unpicking
Table**, usually a broad bench in a settlement laundry or repair room, where a cutter takes it apart
in the company of whoever brought it. Sound panels become patch cloth; narrow strips become ties
and binding; worn fibre becomes stuffing or cleaning rag. Closures, cord, labels, and any thread
long enough to wind are sorted into shallow trays. There is always a jar for bent fasteners that
someone believes can still be straightened, and every unpicking room has an argument about when
that jar is finally full.

The work is public because the garment may carry more than material value. A cutter asks before
opening a partner's repair or separating a child's name from a collar. Families sometimes keep one
useless scrap and surrender the useful pieces, an arrangement practical people pretend not to
understand and honour without question. What remains enters the settlement's repair stock, where a
coat may end as parts of a curtain, a tool roll, and several strangers' cuffs.

### Witness seams {#witness-seams}

Cloth transferred from a recognisable garment often keeps a **witness seam**: a short run of the
old stitching left visible inside the new piece. It tells a future tailor that the patch had a life
before this one and prevents a valuable repair from being mistaken for careless work. Witness seams
are common when an heirloom is divided among relatives, but they appear just as often on ordinary
second-hand clothes. Removing one without asking is not a crime; it is the sort of discourtesy a
family remembers.

There is no rule about which seam must survive. One person preserves a neat line made by a parent;
another keeps the crooked first repair they managed alone. Tailors complain that clients choose the
least structurally convenient stitches and then build the whole alteration around them. They do so
anyway.

## The asynchronous season

Fashion crosses the dark at two speeds, like everything else. A cut, a pattern, a way of wearing a
collar — those are information, and they ride the broadcast tier: a style can sweep the settled
worlds in weeks as pure description. But the cloth itself, the actual made garment, travels by ship.
So every fashion arrives everywhere twice — first as news, then as freight — months apart, and by
the time the garments arrive the local tailors have already built their own version from the
description, better suited to local cloth and local weather. The style comes back changed, and both
versions keep the name. Who wore it first is unanswerable across the lag, and argued anyway, with
pleasure.

Formality is owned by occasions, not wealth. Nearly everyone keeps one good garment — the arrival
coat, most worlds call it — worn for arrivals and departures, for weddings, and for the Sending of
the Dead: the moments when the settled worlds dress up are exactly the moments someone crosses a
threshold. Festival dress is light-work: Lumen garments carry lamplight thread and polished
closures, made to be seen by lamplight in the year's warmest dark.

## Clothes with more than one wearer

### The Growing Rail {#the-growing-rail}

Children's clothes spend very little time belonging to one child. Settlement schools and mutual-aid
stores keep a **Growing Rail**, arranged by size and environment rather than age. Sleeves and hems
are made with deep reserves that can be let out, while knees and seats are expected to be replaced.
A family returns what no longer fits and takes what does. Keeping a sound coat in a drawer while
another child needs it is considered worse manners than accepting one with a crowded history.

That history is written inside the collar. Each wearer adds a small name or household mark without
removing the earlier ones, until a popular garment carries a narrow roll of settlement childhood.
Children compare lists, find the older people who wore their clothes, and complain when a sibling
inherits the coat with the better names. The marks are not ownership records; the rail owns nothing
and remembers everyone.

### Station uniform shells {#station-uniform-shells}

On stations, the durable outer part of a work uniform belongs to the post. **Uniform shells** are
issued, refitted, and returned, with role tabs, pocket panels, and safety markings made to be
replaced without sacrificing the whole garment. The inside service strip records previous wearers
and major repairs. A newly promoted worker may receive an older shell than the one they returned;
rank is in the tab, not in the freshness of the cloth.

The last duty is to clear the pockets. Meal tokens, pencils, screws, and folded notes are taken to
the stores desk and matched to their owner when possible. Every station has a story about something
important found in a returned shell. Most of the actual finds are sweet wrappers and washers, but
the pockets are checked carefully all the same.

## Colour, copying, and useful mistakes

### The Dye Ladder {#the-dye-ladder}

Water, mordants, and colour stock are too dear to spend on one exact shade at a time. Settlement
dye rooms work a shared bath as a **Dye Ladder**, taking the palest cloth first and darkening the
bath as it weakens or muddies. Households book a rung rather than a guaranteed colour. The sample
strip pinned beside the vat is a promise of direction, not a match, and anyone demanding otherwise
is invited to price a fresh bath themselves.

One ladder can put half a corridor into related colours without producing two identical garments.
People notice which neighbours shared a bath, and local names attach to the whole family of shades.
The palest rung is coveted because it shows the cleanest colour; the darkest is defended because it
hides the next repair. Both claims are made as if they were moral principles.

### The Two-World Sleeve {#the-two-world-sleeve}

The **Two-World Sleeve** began as a way to save a coat whose working arm was ruined: one sleeve is
rebuilt from narrow, contrasting strips while the sound sleeve is left alone. A relay description
said the pieced side should face the work. Forge cutters took that to mean the tool arm; Halcyon
cutters put it on the arm that braces and carries. Each version saved cloth, each looked deliberate,
and neither side accepted correction when finished examples eventually crossed.

The disagreement is now part of the cut. Forge and Halcyon tailors demonstrate opposite sleeves
and accuse each other of dressing backwards, while other worlds choose whichever side of the coat
needs repair. A new Two-World Sleeve made from an untouched pair of sleeves is widely judged to have
missed the point.
