# 52-sports.md — games, circuits & the stitched season

> **Cornerstone file — how the settled worlds play, and how sport crosses the lag.** This is the
> `sport` domain; the general place of games in ordinary life is in `50-daily-life.md` ("Games and
> the circuits"), and this file pins the structures. See `README.md` for the authoring conventions.

## The games themselves

Every world plays the old games bent to its own physics, and one game belongs to the void itself. **Hollowball** is the stations' sport: played in the open air of a rotation hub where the spin-gravity fades to nothing, two crews of five contesting a ball that never falls. Every station's hub is a different shape, so every station's court is different — visiting teams learn the hollow before they learn the opponent, and the home advantage is architectural. Planet-dwellers find it beautiful and unwatchable in equal measure; station children play it before they can properly walk in gravity.

The racing circuits are the other great spectacle, and they come in three kinds. Atmospheric racing threads fliers through real weather — Meridian's league is the famous one, run in the calm seasons between the storms. Ice racing belongs to Cold Harbor's long night, engines singing on the frozen sea. And the orbital sprints run ship-against-ship over fixed courses between stations, the nearest thing the between has to a stadium. Racing pilots take call-names in the frontier manner — short, functional, earned — and a pilot's call-name outlives their career.

## Route leagues and port series

### The Route Leagues {#route-leagues}

Hollowball is organised in **Route Leagues**, not geographic divisions. A league is a loop of settlements that ordinary freight and passenger ships can actually connect; every club meets the others on that loop at home and away when berth windows permit. A club can be close enough to hear another world's broadcasts and still belong to a different league because no one can carry a squad between them. League membership changes only after the route itself has proved dependable, so the table has a shape supporters can remember even while individual fixtures slide.

### Port Series {#port-series}

Travel is too expensive for a visiting side to cross the dark for a single match. When a club docks, the host schedules a **Port Series**: a block of fixtures against the local side and any route rivals whose passages can be made to meet. The visitors sleep in school rooms, crew bunks, and borrowed flats; the host canteen serves the away table first, a point of etiquette enforced more fiercely than some rules of play. A postponed fixture is usually folded into the next viable series rather than awarded on paper.

### The Filter Cup {#filter-cup}

The best-known frontier fixture is the **Filter Cup**, contested whenever the Far Reach Beacons meet the Breathe Easy Scrubbers. The trophy is an old air-plant filter housing with handles welded to it, ugly enough that both clubs insist it belongs in the other's cabinet. Beacons supporters bring hand lamps; Scrubbers answer by rattling lengths of clean duct, and broadcasts lower the crowd channel only when speech becomes impossible. The match can count toward a Route League table, but the cup changes hands on the match itself.

## The season across the lag

No two settlements can play on the same day, so the settled worlds long ago stopped pretending. A season is not a calendar; it is a ledger. Fixtures are agreed by relay months ahead, played under local skies, and the results cross the dark on the broadcast tier — hours to the core, a day or two to the far frontier. Records travel slower: a record is provisional until the standing records committee, which convenes by relay between Games years, has ratified the conditions, and ratification takes weeks. Every fan knows the taste of a provisional triumph.

### The Split Table {#split-table}

Every official league table is a **Split Table**, with a reported tally beside a ratified tally. The first changes when a result reaches the station; the second changes only when the match record, officials' marks, and playing conditions have cleared review. Nothing is silently overwritten. If a ruling alters the order, rec-hall boards cross out the old arithmetic but leave it legible, and the sports desk explains the movement from one column to the other.

### The Bay Table {#bay-table}

Racing publishes two standings from the same finishes: one for pilots and one for registered maintenance crews. The crew list is called the **Bay Table**, and it follows the mechanics even when different pilots take the craft out. A bay can lead while none of its pilots does, which is why racing interviews so often happen under a raised engine with someone still holding a spanner. Pilot transfers change one table immediately; a crew's points remain with its bay.

The station's sports desk is what makes the scattered results one season. It stitches fixtures, standings, and commentary into a single running story, always naming the lag as it goes — a race call is a retelling minutes or hours after the fact, and the desk says so with pride rather than apology. Supporters cheer month-old victories at full volume; the wagering is small, constant, and universal, run on honour ledgers in every rec hall, and a settlement that welched on its sporting debts would sooner default on a trade contract.

Teams carry their settlement's name and a working emblem — a tool, a weather, a trade — so the fixture list reads like a map of who the worlds are. Most sport is amateur: shift leagues, children's leagues, the crowded gymnasium after work. The professionals are few and beloved, and a settlement's team is part of its name.

### The Shift Shield {#shift-shield}

Every established settlement keeps a **Shift Shield**, an open local tournament entered by work rota rather than by club. Fixtures are fitted around handovers, and a side that cannot field safely after an emergency shift may reschedule without forfeiting. The shield itself lives at the winners' workplace until the next tournament — propped above a tool cage, strapped inside a farm cart, or hanging in a galley where every dent acquires a disputed story. It is the result most likely to matter to people who will never appear on an inter-settlement table.

## The Inter-Settlement Games

The Games are held each fourth year, hosted by a different world in turn, and hosting is an honour a world prepares a decade for — the bid, the building, the training of a generation of local officials. Events run in the four gravity classes — heavy, standard, light, and spun — with records kept per class, so a heavy-world lifter and a light-world flier are each champion on their own terms. The opening custom is the Reading of the Absent: the names of the settlements too far, too poor, or too newly founded to send anyone, read aloud so the Games begin by remembering who is missing. Athletes travel months to compete; some spend the better part of the four years in transit and training, and the ones who make that crossing more than once are honoured for the travelling as much as the winning.
