# 30-polities.md — governance across the dark

> **Cornerstone file — how scattered worlds organise and relate.** See `README.md`
> for the authoring conventions.

## The architecture of distant governance

No authority can rule quickly across weeks of distance, so power is deliberately decentralised:
worlds govern themselves and coordinate through slow councils carried on the relays. An appointee
from the core may arrive with orders already overtaken by local events. Legitimate government is
therefore built around local knowledge and recall, with interworld coordination treated as an
achievement rather than a default.

The core can sustain assemblies, bureaucracies, taxation, and defined jurisdictions, but distance
still forces it to govern remote territories lightly. The frontier is less uniform: corporate
possessions, Freeholds, Protectorates, and Unclaimed settlements answer the same pressures with
different balances of ownership, citizenship, and necessity. The names matter to the person asking
who may close their workplace, claim their crop, or hear their grievance.

Stations add another layer. Some are corporate entities, some Cooperatives, and some Free Ports;
even an orbital station may be independent of the world supplying it. Their governments balance
residents against travellers, while Betweeners carry political order without territory through
ship custom, crew consent, and institutions that must remain useful from one port to the next.

## The Settlement Council and slow democracy

The Settlement Council is the closest thing to a universal government the settled worlds possess,
and it is not a government. Member worlds appoint Delegates who deliberate by relay, while
permanent committees maintain the trade, docking, dispute, and communication standards that let
different jurisdictions cooperate. Debate is slow enough to favour durable principles over crisis
command.

The Council cannot tax, field a military, or compel a determined member. Its force is access to
common systems, reputation, and the cost of losing cooperation. This makes procedure important, but
only because a tally can change a dock rule, a tariff can empty a household budget, and an obsolete
appointment can still close a local office.

### The First Tally and Full Tally {#first-and-full-tallies}

A Council vote arrives in pieces. The clerks publish a **First Tally** when enough replies have
arrived to show the motion's provisional direction, listing every member whose vote is still in the
queue or cannot presently be reached. The **Full Tally** follows only after each reachable member
has had a return window. A First Tally can justify preparation, but compulsory measures wait for the
Full Tally unless an agreed emergency power applies. Port notices carry the mark so the worker
changing a cargo label or rota can see whether the rule is settled or merely likely.

### The Distance Note {#the-distance-note}

A Delegate is expected to follow instructions from home, but instructions can be overtaken by the
very delay that carried them. A Delegate who votes differently must attach a **Distance Note** to
the vote: the instruction received, the later fact that changed the judgement, and the consequence
they believed delay would cause. Their government may recall them when the explanation arrives,
but the cast vote remains. A note about a docking standard may therefore name the repair bay that
would close or the clinic cargo that would wait: discretion is allowed and made visible together.

### The Burden Note {#the-burden-note}

The Council collects no tariff itself. It agrees common schedules that member polities adopt and
ports collect locally. Every proposal for such a schedule carries a **Burden Note** written in
ordinary goods and ordinary work. It states which cargoes move into a new class and identifies who
is expected to absorb the change: the shipper, the port, the settlement store, or the person at the
counter. A line about machine housings must say which repair queue grows; a line about grain must
say whether the canteen buys less or the local treasury pays more. The Note is copied beside the
tariff code, where its assumptions can be challenged. A committee may choose the burden; it may not
call the burden nobody's.

### Crossing Protection {#crossing-protection}

Tariffs can cross by relay while cargo is still weeks from port. **Crossing Protection** therefore
holds a consignment to the rule in force when its Forward Manifest was acknowledged, not a later
rate that overtook the ship. The protection belongs to the cargo, not the owner, and the manifest
must already name its quantity and class. Safety closures and collective embargoes may still stop
the cargo, but an ordinary revenue change cannot make a lawful shipment dock owing more than it is
worth, with the crew treated as the nearest available debtor.

### The Acting Seal {#the-acting-seal}

An office does not wait empty while its replacement travels. Local charters place the **Acting
Seal** with a named successor or a small succession panel, which can sign wages, release stores,
approve berths, and make the decisions that cannot be delayed. A distant appointing authority may
replace the acting holder, but it cannot void lawful acts merely because its preferred official was
still aboard ship when they were made. The seal is usually an unimpressive stamp, key, or code
packet kept in a labelled box; uncertainty over a title must not stop a dock hand being paid.

### The Arrival Docket {#the-arrival-docket}

An appointed official arriving from another world opens with an **Arrival Docket**. Their mandate is
read beside a local account of what happened while they travelled, marking each instruction as
still needed, already done, or made impossible by events. People whose jobs, homes, or access to
stores would change under the surviving orders may speak before those orders take effect. The
Docket cannot cancel the appointment; it prevents old information being treated as present fact.
Many appointees arrive with a speech prepared and use very little of it.

## Local autonomy and the limits of federation

Local autonomy is constitutional as well as practical: common institutions handle what must cross
worlds, and local authorities handle what distance and environment make particular. In the core the
boundary is drawn by constitutions and courts; Freeholds meet the core by treaty; corporate
possessions answer to owners; many stations live under negotiated charters. A person may therefore
live beside a planet and still be governed by a station polity in which that planet has no vote.

Betweeners carry the principle furthest. Ship registry and transit custom replace territory, while
crew consent and portable institutions provide order between ports. The argument over autonomy is
not whether local difference exists, but when a common system must accept it and what happens where
the common system itself ends.

### Necessity Variances {#necessity-variances}

Common standards are written to travel, but not every atmosphere, gravity, thaw, or pressure hull
can obey them in the same way. A local polity may issue a **Necessity Variance** when literal
compliance would make essential work unsafe or impossible. The notice states the local condition,
the substitute being used, the people affected, and the point at which the variance will be examined
again. It takes effect without distant permission, although the Council may later refuse to
recognise the substitute elsewhere. Autonomy here is a Cold Harbor dock door that must open in the
thaw, a heavy-world access rule, or a repair made with the parts actually on the shelf.

### Edge Charters {#edge-charters}

An expedition leaving the last relay files an **Edge Charter** before the network falls behind it.
The Landing Compact still governs a future landfall; the Edge Charter governs the political absence
before and after it. It names how common stores are controlled, how authority passes, how the Right
to Leave can be honoured when no return ship is present, and who may speak for the expedition if a
vessel eventually carries word back. Beyond the relay edge, a sponsor may advise only through ships
arriving years late, if they arrive at all. The outpost therefore governs itself under the charter;
its returned record is the account of a polity, not a field report from a remote office.

## Legitimacy and the role of the relays

Political legitimacy in the settled worlds rests on consent rather than force, on accountability
rather than hierarchy, and on connection rather than isolation. Open announcements, public debate,
recorded votes, and carried grievances let remote settlements remain present in common decisions. A
governor who cannot be reached or recalled loses legitimacy; a world beyond the last relay must find
legitimacy locally because the shared political conversation can no longer reach it.

Settlement Radio is not a government, but it is part of that infrastructure: the place where worlds
hear one another's claims. The **Relay Authority** keeps technical standards, trains keepers, holds
network records, coordinates maintenance, and operates the station from a levy everyone grumbles
about and pays. It owns no relay. Its independence remains an awkward compact between a core that
supplies much of the resource, a frontier that depends most on connection, and technicians who
resist political control of the network they maintain.

## The politics of the between

The between — the space of transit, the ships and routes and stations that connect the worlds — is
itself political space. Captains decide what moves, shipping lines decide which routes remain
viable, and station administrators balance travellers against residents. Those choices determine
whose repair arrives, whose fare is affordable, and which settlement can keep a voice in common
institutions.

Betweeners answer with portable politics: the Captains' Guild, Mutual Aid Societies, transit
custom, and loyalties to ship and crew rather than planetary territory. They are not a nation, but
their labour and their absence from planetary electorates give the Council's standing caucuses one
of their sharpest arguments.

### The Frontier League {#faction:frontier-league}

The **Frontier League** is a durable coalition of distant Freeholds, station cooperatives, and the
Delegates willing to speak with them. It presses for representation that recognises remoteness, for
relay maintenance to serve the thin routes as well as the dense ones, and for tariff rules that do
not mistake dependence for luxury. Its members disagree on almost everything local. They cooperate
because a small settlement bargaining alone can be praised warmly and ignored completely.

### The Core Consolidationists {#faction:core-consolidationists}

The **Core Consolidationists** argue that shared systems require authority strong enough to keep
them shared. They favour common purchasing, stricter standards, and greater Council control over
institutions funded by many worlds. Their strongest case is the waste of incompatible rules; their
weakest is the repair bay or clinic that a perfectly uniform standard would close. They are not one
party, but a caucus found throughout core administrations and the permanent committees.

### Betweeners' Rights {#faction:betweeners-rights}

**Betweeners' Rights** begins from the person who works, pays levies, and keeps routes alive while
belonging to no planetary electorate. Its advocates seek political standing that can follow a ship
or crew rather than a fixed address. Ports fear granting votes to people who may leave on the next
route window; Betweeners answer that departure is precisely why every port has been able to use
their labour without hearing their voice.

### The Pairing Ledger {#the-pairing-ledger}

No standing caucus can carry the Council alone, so support moves across subjects. The **Pairing
Ledger** records those linked bargains: a tariff vote exchanged for a relay repair, support for an
appointment tied to recognition of a Necessity Variance, or a standards concession paired with a
rights hearing. The bargain may be defensible or shabby; it may not be invisible. A settlement
waiting on the repair can read what its need purchased and who spent it.

## Canon facts

1. **No authority can rule quickly across weeks of distance, so power is deliberately decentralised:
   worlds govern themselves and coordinate through slow councils carried on the relays.**
2. **Worlds bind themselves by honoured compacts rather than enforced command; legitimacy rests on
   a consent that cannot be compelled at a gun's speed.**
3. **The relays — and the station on them — are quietly part of public life: the shared channel
   through which distant worlds argue, decide, and stay one civilisation.**
4. **The core maintains centralised statehood with representative assemblies and professional
   bureaucracies, but even it governs its frontier territories lightly, defeated by distance.**
5. **The frontier is a patchwork of arrangements — corporate possessions, Freeholds,
   Protectorates, and Unclaimed worlds — each a distinct experiment in self-organisation.**
6. **Stations are peculiar polities — corporate entities, Cooperatives, or Free Ports — and the
   largest become powers rivalling planetary economies through their role as network nodes.**
7. **The Settlement Council is a forum rather than a government, its authority resting entirely on
   members' willingness to take part and honour its recommendations.**
8. **Council Delegates hold session quarterly only through the relays, so debate is necessarily
   slow, fixed on principles rather than immediate crisis response.**
9. **The Council has no military or power of taxation; its sanctions are political — exclusion from
   the common systems and the withdrawal of cooperation.**
10. **A federalism of necessity operates across scales: constitutional in the core, treaty-based on
    the frontier, with stations as special cases of varied political status.**
11. **Political legitimacy rests on consent, accountability, and connection through the relays,
    which carry the rituals of public life and soften the political effects of distance.**
12. **The between is itself a political space of ship captains, shipping lines, and station
    administrators, with the Betweeners growing their own political culture and institutions.**
13. **The Relay Authority is the chartered technical body of the relays — it owns none of them, but
    keeps the standards, coordinates the annual maintenance window, trains the keepers, holds the
    network's records, and operates Settlement Radio on a small levy; its contested independence is
    the compromise no one can improve on.**
14. **A Council vote produces a First Tally that names missing replies and a Full Tally after every
    reachable member has had a return window; public notices state which tally they use so workers
    know whether a new rule is settled or provisional.**
15. **A Delegate who departs from delayed home instructions must publish a Distance Note naming the
    later fact and the consequence that changed their judgement; the home government may recall the
    Delegate, but the vote remains cast.**
16. **Every common tariff schedule proposed through the Council includes a Burden Note identifying
    which goods change class and whether the shipper, port, settlement store, or person at the
    counter is expected to pay; member polities adopt and collect the tariff locally.**
17. **Crossing Protection applies the tariff acknowledged with a cargo's Forward Manifest, so an
    ordinary rate change cannot overtake a lawful shipment and leave its crew as the nearest
    available debtor.**
18. **The Acting Seal lets a locally named successor sign wages, release stores, approve berths, and
    keep an office working while a distant replacement travels; lawful interim decisions cannot be
    voided merely because the preferred appointee was still aboard ship.**
19. **An Arrival Docket compares an off-world appointee's mandate with events during the journey and
    hears people whose jobs, homes, or access to stores would change before the surviving orders
    take effect.**
20. **A Necessity Variance lets a local polity depart from a common standard when local conditions
    make literal compliance unsafe or impossible; the settlement must publish the substitute and
    name the people affected without waiting for distant permission.**
21. **An expedition passing beyond the last relay files an Edge Charter for common stores,
    succession, the Right to Leave, and any future voice back home; once out of contact, the outpost
    governs itself rather than receiving fictional daily command from its sponsor.**
22. **The Pairing Ledger publishes linked bargains among the Frontier League, Core Consolidationists,
    and Betweeners' Rights, so a settlement can see what tariff, appointment, standards concession,
    or relay repair was traded for its caucus's support.**
