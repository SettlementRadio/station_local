# 72-celebrity.md — names, reputations & the public circuit

> **Cornerstone file — how a private person becomes a public name across the relay lag.** Living
> musicians, racers, presenters and officials belong to generated world state; this file pins the
> durable customs around them and the dead figures whose reputations have become part of the
> culture. Settlement Radio's own presenters remain working voices rather than conventional
> celebrities, as fixed in `../core/STATION.md`. See `README.md` for the authoring conventions.

## A name crosses faster than a person

Fame has a geography. A singer may fill every hall on Meridian and still be known on Cold Harbor
only as a voice copied from a weak relay signal. A racing pilot may be a household call-name along
one circuit and a line in the results elsewhere. A Council delegate can be admired on Concordance,
disliked on the frontier and entirely unknown at Far Reach. No public reputation arrives whole.
Recordings, reports and arguments move ahead of the person; by the time the person follows, the
audience has already made a character from the fragments.

The wide broadcast tier gives public names unusual reach, but not a single shared present. An
appearance notice can cross the settled worlds quickly. The performance itself may reach some
listeners later in a clean recording, others as a damaged copy, and others only through a review.
A correction can spend days chasing the claim it corrects. Public figures learn to live beside
several versions of themselves, each true to a different audience for a little while.

Most fame remains local. The person who calls the ice races, the singer who keeps the storm-season
dance hall full, the patient chair of a port inquiry: these people can matter intensely without
ever becoming network names. The settled worlds do not treat that as lesser fame. A reputation is
measured by who relies on it, not only by how far it travels. Network fame is simply the rare case
in which enough local audiences recognise the same name.

### The Relay List {#relay-list}

The Relay List is the shared public packet from which newsrooms build their lighter pages and
programmes. It carries confirmed appearance notices, release announcements, race entries, formal
statements, corrections and rights-cleared extracts from interviews. It is assembled cooperatively
by local desks and repeated over the public channels; no office owns the whole list, and every
world cuts its own edition. To have "made the List" means only that a name is being discussed in
more than one place. It is not a ranking, an award or a guarantee that the discussion is kind.

The List is deliberately plain. An entry says what happened, where the report came from and when a
reply was requested. Commentary is added by the receiving desk, which is why the same quarrel can
sound comic on one world and grave on another. False notices do get through, but a desk that passes
too many of them soon finds its contributions carried with a warning or omitted. Reputation keeps
the reputation business honest more effectively than any distant authority could.

### The Reply Mark {#reply-mark}

The Reply Mark is the customary notation placed beside a public claim about someone who could not
answer before transmission. It obliges no one, but reputable broadcasters preserve the mark and
give the eventual answer comparable placement. A feud may therefore unfold in layers: accusation,
reply requested, reply received, clarification, weary final note. Listeners become skilled at
hearing which layer they have. The custom does not stop gossip; it stops the lag from turning
absence into consent.

## The public circuit

Physical appearances matter because bodies still travel at the speed of ships. A well-known
musician does not skim through a string of settlements for brief engagements. They take a long
route, stay long enough to work with local players, give several performances and leave behind a
recording that will travel farther than they can. A cancelled arrival is therefore not a diary
inconvenience but an empty hall, a stranded technical crew and months of work to rearrange. Good
coverage names those people as readily as the missing star.

### The Arrival Rail {#arrival-rail}

At larger ports, the Arrival Rail is the ordinary barrier between the public gallery and the
working dock. When a notable visitor lands, the first appearance usually happens there: coat still
creased from transit, cargo moving behind them, port staff trying to keep the gangway clear. The
custom began because there was nowhere else to put the crowd. It survived because the setting is
honest. A polished hall can be arranged later; at the rail, the audience sees that the famous
person crossed the same dark as everyone else.

Musicians build reputations through recordings, credits and the company they keep. Listeners know
who wrote a piece, who played on it, which local workshop built the instrument and whether the
headline performer remembers any of those names in public. Feuds most often begin around credit,
money, missed appearances or a style borrowed without acknowledgement. They are followed with
pleasure when the stakes are professional and with discomfort when real harm is involved. The
station keeps the difference clear.

Racers are known by earned call-names, but a call-name belongs partly to a crew. Mechanics, weather
readers and course officials appear in the stories because no race is won by a pilot alone. Fans
remember conduct as closely as results: who thanked the repair bay, who challenged a provisional
ruling without insulting the officials, who appeared at a shift-league practice after the main
event. A champion's result changes the standings; their habits make the reputation.

Officials become public figures almost against the nature of their work. The slow councils produce
recognisable voices: the delegate whose replies are always late but careful, the tribunal chair who
can make hostile parties answer the same question, the port administrator who explains an ugly
decision without hiding behind procedure. Their fame attaches to recorded votes, hearings and
consequences rather than private life. The public may gossip about a famous official's manner, but
the record is what allows the reputation to survive a change of office.

Presenters occupy an intimate category. A local announcer may be the most familiar voice in a
settlement while remaining unrecognised in its corridors. Settlement Radio carries that intimacy
much farther, yet its tradition resists turning the crew into ornaments: listeners know voices,
preferences, verbal habits and pieces of approved biography, not a managed display of private
life. A presenter can still become the subject of a light item after an on-air mistake, a public
appearance or a notable interview. The story is about the work and the shared joke, not access to
the person behind it.

Public tokens are small because mass is expensive. Signed cards, copied greetings, locally printed
song sheets and cloth patches travel more easily than elaborate goods. Often only the design
crosses the relay and each settlement makes its own version from available material. The variations
become part of the pleasure: a Meridian racing patch and its Cold Harbor copy may share a call-name
and almost nothing else.

## Names the record keeps

Living public figures change too quickly for the bible. The nightly world record supplies their
names, work, affiliations, current appearances and quarrels. Canon keeps only people safely in the
past, when the reputation itself has become a durable fact. These old names are not templates for
every new celebrity; they show what the settled worlds tend to remember after results, releases and
office have moved on.

### Ceryn Daro, called Turnglass {#ceryn-daro-turnglass}

Ceryn Daro was a Meridian atmospheric racer whose call-name, **Turnglass**, became better known
than her given name. She won often enough to fill the archives, but the story repeatedly told is a
loss: after a disputed storm-course finish, Daro refused to claim a provisional record and spent
the interview naming the weather readers and repair crew who had got the field home. The record was
later denied. Her reputation improved. Long after her death, a racer who credits the whole bay
before discussing their own result is said to have "turned the glass."

### Adra Pell and Lio Tern {#adra-pell-and-lio-tern}

Adra Pell and Lio Tern were relay-pop partners who made their name recording apart, passing verses
between two settlements until a song was complete. Their public quarrel began over an altered
credit on a frontier pressing and grew through open letters, cancelled sessions and supporters who
treated every new lyric as evidence. Both are long dead, and the argument is now safer than it was
while they lived. Their last shared recording contains no spoken introduction and no settled
account of whether they reconciled. Music programmes still play it from both sides of the dispute.

### Edda Sol, the Late Voice {#edda-sol-late-voice}

Edda Sol presented the overnight service on Breathe Easy when the station's old air plant still
deserved its name. Sol read maintenance notices, dock arrivals, meal rotas and listener letters in
the same steady register, and recordings of those ordinary programmes spread after the equipment
was finally replaced. She was famous without a public image and rarely travelled. The Late Voice
is remembered not for a revelation or scandal but for making routine information sound like the
work of a community.

### The Edda Sol Citation {#edda-sol-citation}

Local presenters receive the Edda Sol Citation for useful broadcasting that attracts no spectacle.
The citation is decided by working presenters in another settlement and carries no money, only a
recording drawn from the recipient's ordinary output and added to the public archive.

### Dar Olan, the Long Chair {#dar-olan-long-chair}

Dar Olan chaired standards hearings for the Settlement Council and became unexpectedly famous for
letting every affected port finish its evidence, however repetitive the relay exchange became. His
running dispute with a Halcyon delegate over docking rules was followed as entertainment, though
the eventual standard mattered far more than either man. Olan is long dead; an inquiry that takes
too long but leaves no party unheard is still called an Olan sitting. The phrase can be praise or a
complaint, usually both.

## What a public name is owed

Celebrity coverage is light in manner, not careless in substance. Work, appearances, public
statements, professional rivalries and harmless vanities are fair material. Private grief, medical
details, family members who did not seek attention and the suffering behind a public event are not
converted into colour. A newsroom that cannot tell the difference loses contributors first and
listeners soon after.

There is no official table of fame. Chart positions come from airplay, standings come from results,
and offices come from their own political processes; celebrity borrows from all three but governs
none of them. The Relay List records attention without pretending to measure affection. A name may
be discussed everywhere for an embarrassing reason and loved deeply in only one place.

Retreat is possible. A performer can stop issuing recordings, a racer can leave the circuit, an
official can return to private work, and a presenter can finish a microphone tour. Old material
remains in archives, but reputable desks distinguish an archived public record from a new claim on
someone's attention. The settled worlds remember long, yet they understand that remembrance is not
ownership.
