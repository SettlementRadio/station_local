# 20-peoples.md — the branches of humanity

> **Cornerstone file — who the settled humans are now.** See `README.md` for the
> authoring conventions.

## The heavy-worlders

The heavy-worlders are those who live where gravity pulls harder than Earth ever did. Their bodies
are shorter, denser, their bones thicker, their hearts more powerful. They move slowly,
deliberately, conserving energy in an environment that punishes waste. On standard-gravity worlds
they are uncomfortable, their joints aching, their balance uncertain, their bodies built for a pull
that no longer holds them. They are a reminder that human form is not fixed, that the body answers
its conditions, that what is "normal" is only ever local.

The heavy-worlders have grown cultures that prize endurance and deliberation. Their art runs to the
monumental, their music to the slow and deep, their dealings to the formal and sustained. Others see
them as dour, patient, reliable — sometimes as plodding, unimaginative, stuck. They see themselves
as grounded, literal, rooted in a physical reality their lighter cousins can afford to ignore. The
heavy-worlds are generally poor, dependent on trade for what they cannot make, their exports mostly
raw materials and the labour of their strong bodies. Their resentment of the lighter worlds is old
and deep.

## The light-worlders

The light-worlders live where gravity is gentler than Earth-standard — on small planets, moons,
orbital habitats where the body moves freely, where the effort of living is less. Their bodies are
taller, thinner, their bones more fragile, their muscles less developed. They move quickly,
gracefully, their gestures wide in the space that light gravity allows. On standard or heavy worlds
they are weak, their hearts labouring, their bones at risk under loads they were never built to
bear.

The light-worlders have grown cultures that prize speed and reach. Their art runs to the aerial,
the mobile, the ephemeral; their music is fast, their dealings quick and intense, their attention
moving rapidly from one thing to the next. Others see them as flighty, superficial, impatient —
sometimes as free, creative, unburdened. They see themselves as liberated, their bodies released
from the tyranny of weight that binds their heavier cousins. The light-worlds are often wealthy,
their orbital stations centres of manufacturing and research, their populations educated and
mobile. Their disdain for the heavy-worlders is casual and pervasive.

## The Betweeners

The Betweeners are those who live in the ships and stations, in the transit corridors and the relay
outposts, never settling on any world. They are born in zero gravity, or in the spun gravity of
rotation, their bodies shaped to conditions that planet-dwellers find alien. They are taller than
heavy-worlders, more compact than light-worlders, their bodies answering the particular demands of
their environments: the need to move in three dimensions, to conserve resources, to endure the
radiation and isolation of the void.

The Betweeners have grown a culture that prizes adaptability and self-sufficiency. They are the
traders, the pilots, the engineers, the go-betweens of the worlds. They speak a dialect that blends
the tongues of a dozen planets, they keep customs that are portable, they form relationships intense
and temporary, suited to the brief encounters of transit. Planet-dwellers see them as rootless,
mercenary, untrustworthy — sometimes as cosmopolitan, sophisticated, free. They see themselves as
the truest humans of the settled worlds, the ones who have adapted most fully to existence between
the stars, who have made a home of the void itself. It is among the Betweeners that the
journey-is-destination conviction runs deepest — held as outright faith only by the small Wayfarer
creed, but present as a temperament in many who live in transit: the sense that the crossing, not
the arrival, is the point.

The Betweeners include the Children of the Signal, those who have chosen to live in the relay
stations, keeping the network that binds the worlds together. They are a subculture within the
Betweeners — more isolated, more dedicated, stranger. They speak of the relays as living things,
they grow rituals around the maintenance of equipment, they form communities that span the distance
between stations through their shared labour and their shared listening.

## The world-bound and the world-lost

Most humans still live on worlds — on planets with natural gravity and atmosphere, however
modified. They are the world-bound, those for whom the planetary surface is still the default, the
natural, the proper place for human life. They are as various as the worlds themselves: the people
of desert planets, of ocean worlds, of forest moons, of volcanic rock barely habitable. Each world
has shaped its people, and each people has shaped its world — the slow loop of adaptation and
terraforming producing kinds of human experience the first settlers could never have foreseen.

### Meridian storm households {#meridian-storm-households}

On Meridian's storm coast, a sealed building becomes a **storm household** for as long as the
weather holds it shut. Residents, visitors, stranded shift workers, and the person who only came to
repair a latch share meals, cleaning, care work, and the negotiation of who may make noise where.
The arrangement ends when the doors open, but its language does not. People who were shut in
together call one another storm-kin and are expected to offer each other first shelter at a later
closure. A storm-cousin may be otherwise unknown to the family and still appear on an invitation
between blood relatives.

### The Night Answer {#the-night-answer}

Cold Harbor keeps the **Night Answer**: a voice heard on a local channel during the long night is
acknowledged before anyone asks who is calling or why. The answer may come from a work hut, a racing
shed, or a household that can offer nothing beyond confirmation that somebody heard. Cold Harbor
people carry the reflex elsewhere. They answer unattended room speakers, reply to test calls, and
occasionally say *heard* to an announcement meant for another deck. Other worlds tease them for it;
they find the alternative difficult to explain.

### The Forge knock {#the-forge-knock}

Forge people test a door, chair, rail, or unfamiliar work surface with their knuckles before they
put weight on it. The **Forge knock** began in workshops where a change in sound could matter and
escaped into ordinary manners long ago. Visitors from Forge knock dining tables and garden gates
without noticing; hosts elsewhere sometimes mistake it for criticism of the workmanship. Forge
children are taught to say thank you before testing something in another person's home, which
prevents no misunderstandings at all.

### Ashfall's Second Address {#ashfalls-second-address}

Every Ashfall household keeps a **Second Address** in another part of the rebuilt settlement: a
named household with sleeping space, copies of essential records, and an obligation to receive them
if their own district closes. The arrangement is reciprocal but rarely equal; a large family may be
paired with one quiet elder who complains about the stored bedding and renews it before anyone can
ask. Children learn both routes and write both addresses on school forms. To give only one is to
sound either newly arrived or far too confident about a volcanic world.

The world-lost are those who have left their natal worlds and cannot go back. The heavy-worlder who
moves to a standard world finds their body failing; the light-worlder who settles on a planet finds
themselves trapped by gravity; the Betweener who tries to settle finds themselves restless, unable
to sleep in a bed that does not move, unable to bear the open sky. The world-lost are a growing
population, the casualties of the very mobility the settled worlds permit. They gather in the
stations, in the orbital habitats, in the places that come closest to the conditions they have
adapted to. They are a reminder that adaptation does not reverse, that the human form is plastic but
not endlessly so, that the price of variety is sometimes exile from a whole range of human
experience.

## Kinship, gender & the shapes of belonging

Bodies are not the only thing the worlds reshaped. Kinship and gender vary as widely as gravity
does. Some worlds keep the old forms — the couple, the bloodline, the family name carried down.
Others count kin by household rather than blood, or by ship and crew, so that a Betweener
introducing their "mother" may mean the navigator who raised them between ports. Several worlds
have grown social genders the old words never anticipated — roles named for work, for season, for
the stage of a life — and a traveller learns to ask rather than assume. None of this is treated as
exotic in the settled worlds; it is treated like dialect. Each world's arrangements are locally
obvious, mutually strange, and translatable with patience, and the station's DJs — who speak to all
of them at once — long ago learned the grammar of asking.

## The question of the human

The settled worlds have not resolved the question of what human means. The word is used, needed,
heavy with history and hope. But it strains at the edges. Are the heavy-worlders and the
light-worlders the same kind? They can have children together, but those children are often frail,
caught between adaptations that do not combine well. Are the Betweeners human in the same way as
the world-bound, with cultures that planet-dwellers can barely follow, bodies shaped to conditions
that would kill their ancestors? Are the Children of the Signal still human, with their rituals and
their isolation, their bodies marked by the radiation of the relays?

The legal answer is that all are human, owed the rights and protections of the Compacts. The
biological answer is that the settled worlds hold a spread of variation that earlier ages would
have called the beginning of speciation. The cultural answer is that humanity is a choice, a
commitment, a way of being that is learned and kept rather than simply given. The practical answer
is that the worlds must deal with one another — must trade, must speak, must find ways to recognise
kinship across differences that once would have seemed absolute.

The prejudices of the settled worlds follow these lines of difference. The heavy-worlders look down
on the light-worlders as weak, decadent, overreaching. The light-worlders look down on the
heavy-worlders as crude, limited, stuck in the mud. The planet-dwellers look down on the Betweeners
as rootless, amoral, not fully human. The Betweeners look down on the planet-dwellers as provincial,
bound by gravity and air, unable to see the larger picture. These prejudices are not universal —
there are individuals who rise above them, communities that mix, places where the lines blur — but
they are stubborn, shaping the meetings of peoples, the politics of the Settlement Council, the
patterns of migration and settlement.

## Canon facts

1. **After centuries apart, humanity has branched: each world's gravity, light, and air shaped its
   settlers, so "human" now spans a wide spectrum.**
2. **The worlds regard one another with a mix of kinship and wariness — fascination across distance,
   prejudice up close, and a shared word that strains to hold them all.**
3. **The people of the ships and the relays, born or raised in the between, are a culture of their
   own — at home in transit.**
4. **Heavy-worlders have grown shorter, denser bodies and cultures that prize endurance and
   deliberation, often seen by others as dour and reliable.**
5. **Light-worlders have grown taller, thinner bodies and cultures that prize speed and reach,
   often seen by others as flighty and creative.**
6. **The Betweeners include the Children of the Signal, a subculture who keep the relay stations
   alive with their own rituals and isolation.**
7. **The world-lost are those who can no longer return to their natal worlds, their bodies adapted
   past reversal, gathering in stations and orbital habitats.**
8. **Legal, biological, cultural, and practical definitions of "human" coexist uneasily across the
   settled worlds' spread of variation.**
9. **Prejudices follow the lines of difference — heavy against light, planet against void — shaping
   politics and the patterns of migration.**
10. **Kinship and gender take different shapes on different worlds — blood-kin, household-kin,
    crew-kin, and social genders the old words never anticipated — treated across the worlds like
    dialect: locally obvious, mutually strange, translatable with patience.**
11. **On Meridian's storm coast, everyone sealed in one building during a closure forms a storm

12. **Cold Harbor's Night Answer requires a voice on a local channel during the long night to be

13. **Forge people knock on an unfamiliar door, chair, rail, or work surface before trusting it with
