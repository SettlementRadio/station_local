# 36-logistics.md — freight, ports & the carrying trade

> **Cornerstone file — how people and cargo move through the settled worlds.** `35-economy.md`
> explains why trade matters and `78-communication.md` explains how word travels; this file covers
> the freight road itself: convoys, ports, cargo priorities, missed connections and the people who
> carry the mass. Logistics is people and cargo under delay, never a recital of schedules. See
> `README.md` for the authoring conventions.

## The freight road

There is no useful straight line between most settlements. Ships follow lanes opened by orbital
motion, call at stations where cargo can be combined, and wait for the next tractable route. A box
from Forge to Far Reach may cross several docks and several crews without anyone involved making
the whole journey. The route is held together by marks, manifests and handovers: plain records that
say where the box came from, where it is meant to go and who accepted responsibility at the last
port.

Freight therefore moves in pulses. A port can be quiet enough for children to play beside the
sealed freight hall, then suddenly short of berths, lifting gear and rested workers when a route
opens and several ships arrive together. Local shops adjust their work around those pulses.
Boarding houses fill. Canteens put on the large pots. Repair bays defer anything that can safely
wait. When the ships leave, the port settles back to maintenance and paperwork, with a floor full
of scuff marks and a list of things that missed the lift.

### The Forward Manifest {#forward-manifest}

The Forward Manifest is the cargo record sent by relay ahead of a ship. It gives the receiving
port enough notice to reserve a berth, assemble the right handling crew, prepare storage and warn
the people waiting for essential goods. Because an addressed manifest rides the relay queue, it
does not always arrive early. Sometimes the ship and its first manifest reach the dock together;
sometimes several corrected versions have already caused the port to rearrange itself repeatedly.

Dock workers call the first version a promise and the later versions the weather. A correction may
mean a cold room cleared for cargo that is no longer aboard, a specialist called off shift for a
machine that went elsewhere, or a family waiting at the wrong lock for a passenger who changed
ships. The manifest is indispensable and never mistaken for the physical truth. The receiving
loadmaster reads it against what actually crosses the hatch.

A missed connection spreads outward. Cargo loses its next ship, the next ship carries empty space
that cannot be recovered, a repair waits, and the vessel meant to take the repaired part home also
waits. The station reports the people inside that chain: the hydroponics crew stretching old
filters, the dock shift sent home, the cook changing the ration plan. "Delayed freight" is not one
event but a row of ordinary plans that no longer fit.

## Convoys and the shared crossing

Convoys form around a route window, not around identical ships. Grain carriers, tankers, passenger
ships and independent haulers may travel the same lane at different loads and with different
owners. They share navigation reports, watch one another's systems and remain near enough to help
when a vessel has to slow or turn back. Armed escort matters on exposed routes, but most days the
convoy earns its keep through spare skill, compatible tools and another crew awake on the radio.

A convoy is governed by agreement among captains. One vessel may lead through a difficult section
because its navigator knows the route; another may take the relay watch; another may have the best
repair bay. The arrangement lasts for the crossing and dissolves at port. Betweeners are sceptical
of owners who treat cooperation as wasted fuel. A hauler that repeatedly accepts help and never
offers it finds fewer convoys willing to match its departure.

### The Common Hold {#common-hold}

The Common Hold is capacity reserved across a convoy rather than on one particular ship. It carries
shared spares, rescue supplies and cargo displaced when another vessel loses storage or life-support
capacity. The space is contributed unevenly according to what each ship can safely spare, and its
use is recorded for settlement at the destination. It is not charity: it is the convoy insuring
itself with mass and room instead of promises alone.

The most argued-over Common Hold cargo is rarely dramatic. A crate of seals may keep a tanker in
the convoy. Dry food may let a passenger ship reduce strain on a failing galley. Empty pressure
cases may look useless until someone needs to move a damaged instrument safely. Every experienced
freight crew has a story about the item nobody wanted to carry and everybody was grateful to find.

### Burn day {#burn-day}

Burn day is the turnover point of a long crossing, when a hauler changes its working orientation
and begins the braking half of the journey. Cargo teams inspect restraints before the burn and
again after it, then eat together once the ship is steady. Freight crews sing lane-rock over the
engine rhythm, which is why the form treats burn day as its great occasion.

Nothing about the work stops being work. Someone is always assigned to the quiet watch while the
others eat, and someone always discovers a cup, tool or loose personal item that crossed the common
room despite all instructions. Crews keep informal lists of what travelled on burn day and where it
was found. The winning object is the one that causes no damage and the most embarrassment.

## Ports, priorities & the last tonne

A port is an argument between the ship that needs to leave and the settlement that needs what it
carries. Berths, tugs, pressure locks, storage rooms and rested hands are finite. The port caller
keeps the sequence, the loadmasters speak for cargo, ship crews speak for their departure windows,
and local officials speak for needs that may not appear on a commercial manifest. The best ports
do not avoid these conflicts. They make the reasons for their decisions visible.

### The First Lift {#first-lift}

The First Lift is the working party that unloads survival-critical cargo before the rest of an
arrival: food for a short store, air and water-system parts, clinic consignments and anything needed
to keep a damaged ship safe at berth. Commercial ownership does not change, and priority does not
erase payment. It only means an essential crate is not left behind a hall of furniture, instruments
or private orders while people wait for it.

On a small station the First Lift draws workers from outside the dock roster. Teachers, cooks and
off-shift technicians move sealed loads under the loadmaster's direction, then return to their own
work. The arriving crew is fed before the ordinary unloading begins. Ports disagree about almost
everything else, but making a tired carrier argue for a meal is considered evidence of bad management.

### The Last Tonne Board {#last-tonne-board}

The Last Tonne Board decides the final contested portion of outbound capacity when worthy cargo
exceeds the ship's safe load. It is a public port meeting, usually brief and occasionally bitter.
A replacement pump may be weighed against school materials, a cooperative's tools against a trader's
paid booking, a passenger's household goods against stock for a repair bay. The Board cannot make
the capacity larger. Its job is to state whose plan must wait and why.

Every port develops complaints about its Board. Concordance is accused of producing more paper than
cargo. Forge is accused of favouring anything made of metal. Far Reach is accused of putting relay
parts ahead of nearly everything, to which Far Reach replies that without the relay nobody could
complain to them. The minutes travel with the rejected booking so the next port knows the delay was
a decision, not a lost crate.

### The Home Crate {#home-crate}

The Home Crate is the small personal freight allowance carried for each working crew member outside
the commercial manifest. It holds gifts, repaired household objects, local food that will survive
the crossing, paper letters, children's drawings and the awkward requests relatives make of anyone
going near another world. Its mass is limited; its emotional value is not entered in the accounts.

Crews trade space within their Home Crates with elaborate fairness. A navigator may surrender room
for a mechanic's family parcel on one crossing and reclaim it for a musical instrument on the next.
Nothing causes colder feeling than using borrowed space carelessly. When a crew member has no one
to send to, the empty allowance often carries communal mail or a gift for the next station galley.

## Routes with their own habits

The network shares standards, but each freight route acquires a working culture from what it
carries and the conditions at either end. Cargo crews can often identify a route from the way a
load has been packed, the complaints written in the margin and the food tucked into the handover
locker.

### Return iron {#return-iron}

Forge sends out hull sections, reactor housings and heavy machinery. The ships returning bring worn
assemblies, recoverable alloy, failed tools and parts that a frontier workshop could keep alive no
longer. Crews call the return load **return iron**, whatever mixture of metals it actually contains.
Forge workshops sort it into repair, study, salvage and remelting, and send some of it back out in a
different machine. A clean empty hold arriving at Forge is treated less as efficiency than as a
missed opportunity.

### The Cold Harbor Thaw Turn {#cold-harbor-thaw-turn}

The Cold Harbor Thaw Turn begins when the frozen sea opens and surface settlements can move stored
cargo to the orbital port. Goods accumulated through the long night arrive together: local food,
repair work, family parcels, race equipment and the outward loads that will pay for incoming
supplies. Dock shifts expand, spare rooms fill and the Thaw Docket hears the
manifests that did not survive the winter cleanly.

The first outbound ships carry more farewells than ceremony. Some passengers have been waiting
through the closed season; some cargo is already late by every promise attached to it. Cold Harbor
workers mark the last cleared load by knocking ice from their boots against the freight-hall door.
The door is dented, replacing it is always proposed, and the proposal never survives the next thaw.

At Far Reach, a supply arrival recruits almost the whole station. There are too few permanent dock
hands to unload quickly otherwise, and every incoming ship also carries outgoing recyclables,
repairs and people whose next journey begins there. The canteen serves continuously until the hold
is cleared. Children are kept behind the marked line and given the job of counting containers,
which produces several totals and no figure the loadmaster is willing to sign.

The arrival matters even when nothing goes wrong. A new mug in the galley, a replacement boot in
the right size, a message carried physically because it mattered enough to put in a Home Crate:
these are freight facts too. Logistics is the long practice of getting such things to people before
waiting turns inconvenience into harm.

## Canon facts

1. **Most interworld freight changes ships at hub ports, following orbital route windows rather
   than a direct line from origin to destination.**
2. **The Forward Manifest is sent by relay ahead of a ship so the receiving port can reserve a
   berth, crew and storage, but the loadmaster checks every version against what crosses the hatch.**
3. **A missed freight connection delays more than one crate: later ships lose cargo, repair crews
   wait for parts, dock shifts change and ration plans tighten.**
4. **Convoys gather unlike ships around the same route window and share navigation reports, repair
   skill and mutual assistance for the duration of the crossing.**
5. **The Common Hold is cargo capacity reserved across a convoy for shared spares, rescue supplies
   and loads displaced from a vessel that can no longer carry them safely.**
6. **Burn day is the turnover point of a long crossing; freight crews inspect the load around the
   burn, then eat together and sing lane-rock once the ship is steady.**
7. **The First Lift unloads survival-critical cargo before an arrival's ordinary freight, and the
   arriving crew is fed before routine unloading begins.**
8. **The Last Tonne Board is a public port meeting that assigns the final contested outbound
   capacity and records whose cargo must wait and why.**
9. **The Home Crate is each working crew member's small personal freight allowance for gifts,
   repairs, durable food and physical letters; crews trade its limited space among themselves.**
10. **Forge-bound crews call their loads of worn machinery, recoverable alloy and failed tools
    return iron; Forge sorts it for repair, study, salvage or remelting.**
11. **The Cold Harbor Thaw Turn moves cargo stored through the long night to the orbital port when
    the sea opens, bringing outward loads, waiting passengers and disputed manifests together.**
12. **A supply arrival at Far Reach recruits most of the station to unload, with the canteen kept
    open and children counting containers from behind the marked line.**
